## How it works
```
            ┌──────────── modulator ────────────┐          ┌────────────── carrier ───────────────┐
 fm = r·fc ─► Phasor ─► sineTable ─► × env × I ─┼─ radians ─► Phasor + radiansToPhase(pm) ─► sine ─► × env × level ─► out
            └────────────────────────────────────┘          └────────────────────────────────────────┘
```
`y = car.tick(mod.tick())`, one line per sample. Modulator first, same sample. The modulator's
`level` is the index in radians. `radiansToPhase` converts with a double multiply and an int64 cast,
so any offset wraps cleanly into the uint32 phase (D5 pays off: modulation is one integer add).

**FM vs PM, and why PM:** FM adds the modulator to the *frequency*, so the phase receives its integral.
PM adds it to the *phase* directly. For one sine modulator the spectra are identical (I = Δf/fm, and the
modulator is shifted 90°). They differ as soon as the modulator has a DC component (feedback,
asymmetric waveforms, stacked modulators): in FM, DC integrates into a pitch shift that accumulates
forever, while in PM it's a fixed phase offset. PM keeps every patch in tune, which is why the DX7 does it and CFS does too.

## Alternatives rejected
- **Modulation input in cycles** (the curriculum's sketch). Cycles are natural for the phasor, but the FM
  literature, Bessel tables and every synth's "index" are in radians. One unit, at the boundary where people
  type numbers, wins. Conversion happens once, inside `tick()`.
- **Free-running operator phase.** Every note's attack would sound different (the modulator would be at a
  random phase when the index is highest). Key-synced reset on `noteOn()` makes attacks repeatable. A
  free-run option can come later (DX7 has "OSC KEY SYNC").
- **Float multiply in `radiansToPhase`.** Quantizes phase to 512 steps at index 10 (≈ −122 dB). Double is free.
- **Separate `Modulator` and `Carrier` classes.** Same thing, different wiring. Only the algorithm (L1.5)
  knows which is which.

## What surprised me
1. **My own test had a wrap-around bug.** I checked `phase − 0xC0000000u < 256` and it failed with
   4294967267: float π is slightly *larger* than π, so −π/2 lands just *below* 270°, and unsigned subtraction
   wrapped. Comparing as a signed difference fixed it. Unsigned phase arithmetic is great in DSP and a trap in
   comparisons.
2. MSVC ships `std::cyl_bessel_j` (C++17 special math functions). The FM spectrum can be unit-tested
   against the real Bessel functions with no Python and no tables.
3. How cleanly "index decays with amplitude" turns a metallic bell into a pure sine: the centroid lands on
   exactly 200 Hz.

## [THINK] answers
- **Why can a partial get *quieter* as the index rises?** Each partial's amplitude is J_k(I), and Bessel
  functions oscillate: J₀ (the carrier) falls to zero at I = 2.405, goes negative, comes back. Energy isn't
  added, it's *redistributed* among sidebands (total power is constant: Σ J_k² = 1).
- **fc = fm = 1000 Hz, I = 10: highest sideband? Aliasing at 48 kHz?** Significant sidebands reach about
  k ≈ I + 1 = 11, i.e. 12 kHz (Carson: bandwidth 2·fm·(I+1) = 22 kHz around 1 kHz); J_k is below −60 dB by
  k ≈ 16, so ≈ 17 kHz. That's under 24 kHz: no aliasing *here*. But move fc to 4 kHz with the same ratio and the
  same sidebands reach 60+ kHz and fold. FM aliases too (L4.2).
- **Why is the modulator envelope the main expressive control?** Because the modulator's level *is* the
  timbre. A modulator envelope is a filter envelope in disguise (brightness over time), and tying it to the
  amplitude envelope gives the natural "louder = brighter" behaviour of acoustic instruments (brass above).
