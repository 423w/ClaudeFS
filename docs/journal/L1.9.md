# L1.9: Filters I: one-pole, biquads, frequency response (CFS)
*Date started / finished:* 2026-11-17 / 2026-11-17  ·  *Built by:* Claude

## Goal
The two filters every other filter in CFS is measured against: the one-pole (smoother, DC blocker, tone
control) and the RBJ biquad (LP/HP/BP/notch/peak/shelves), with a computed frequency response `H(e^{jω})`
that a test holds to the *measured* response within 0.1 dB.

## Predictions (before building)
1. **Measured vs computed** will agree to far better than 0.1 dB (float rounding is ~1e-7 relative,
   ≈ 1e-6 dB) everywhere except at the notch's null, where both are "minus a lot" and dB is meaningless.
2. **One-pole −3 dB point:** with `a = 1 − e^(−2π·fc/fs)` the −3 dB frequency equals `fc` for low cutoffs
   and drifts *upward* (the filter gets brighter than asked) as `fc` approaches fs/4, because a single pole
   can't reach zero gain at Nyquist the way the analog RC's response falls forever. Guess: ≈ 1 % high at
   1 kHz, ≈ 10–20 % high at 8 kHz (48 kHz).
3. **`a = 2π·fc/fs` instead** (the "obvious" linear guess) will be close below ~1 kHz, then wrong, and for
   `fc > fs/π` the coefficient exceeds 2 and the filter blows up.
4. **DF1 vs TDF2 in float, static coefficients:** at normal cutoffs both are within ~−130 dB of a double
   reference. At a 20 Hz low-pass the coefficients sit near the unit circle (poles at radius ≈ 0.998),
   so float rounding becomes audible-ish noise: I expect TDF2 to be a bit noisier than DF1 (its states hold
   large partial sums), maybe −100 dB vs −110 dB.
5. **Cutoff sweeps:** updating biquad coefficients every 64 samples (the "once per block" shortcut) will
   leave a zipper/buzz tens of dB above per-sample updating when the cutoff moves fast. Per-sample
   updates are smooth at slow rates, but at fast, deep modulation the biquad will produce transients,
   because its state was computed with *old* coefficients (the problem L1.10's SVF solves).

## What actually happened
`fss_render l1.9`, then `python tools/analyze/plot_response.py "renders/l1.9_ir_*.wav" --computed-same-name`.

1. **Measured vs computed: ✅.** The test (`tests/test_filters.cpp`) checks one-pole LP/HP at 3 cutoffs and all
   7 RBJ types × 3 cutoffs × 2 Qs at 40 frequencies each, against the DFT of the filter's own impulse
   response: all within 0.1 dB (linear error < 1e-4 where the response is below −60 dB). The plots
   (`renders/l1.9_ir_*.response.png`) lie on top of each other; the Python overlay reports ≤ 0.0013 dB for
   LP/BP/peak/shelves. Its 0.026 dB (HP, at 10 Hz) and 0.107 dB (notch, next to the null) are the *plot
   script* interpolating FFT bins onto a steep curve, not the filter: the C++ test evaluates the DFT at the
   exact frequencies and passes.
2. **One-pole −3 dB point (exp coefficient):** 20 Hz → 20.0, 1 kHz → 1001.4 (+0.1 %), 4 kHz → +2.4 %,
   8 kHz → **+10.7 %**, 12 kHz → +34 %, 16 kHz → never reaches −3 dB. Direction right, size right at 8 kHz,
   my 1 kHz guess 10× too pessimistic.
3. **Naive `a = 2π·fc/fs`:** +7.4 % at 1 kHz, +48.6 % at 4 kHz, *never −3 dB* at 8 and 12 kHz (a > 1: the
   filter overshoots and its response stops falling), **unstable** at 16 kHz (a = 2.09). Prediction right.
4. **Float precision** (white noise, error RMS relative to the output, 10 s). I split the error in two,
   because the first run showed DF1 and TDF2 *identical* at −55 dB, which can't be rounding noise:

   | LP, Q 0.707 | coefficient quantization | DF1 state noise | TDF2 state noise |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 20 Hz (pole radius 0.99815) | **−55.4 dB** | −69.5 dB | −65.4 dB |
   | 200 Hz | −85.4 dB | −99.4 dB | −95.8 dB |
   | 2 kHz | −118.5 dB | −128.9 dB | −126.8 dB |

   The dominant error at low cutoffs is not noise at all: rounding the *coefficients* to float moves the
   poles, so the float filter is a slightly different filter. DF1's state noise is 2–4 dB below TDF2's at
   every cutoff (direction predicted; my absolute numbers were 30–40 dB too optimistic at 20 Hz).
5. **Sweeps** (the L1.7 FM bass line through a resonant LP):
   - Slow sweep, 150 Hz → 6 kHz → 150 Hz over 20 s, Q 4: per-64-sample coefficient updates differ from
     per-sample by **−69 dB** relative to the output. No zipper worth the name: **prediction wrong** for slow sweeps.
   - Fast wobble, 8 Hz, 150 Hz ↔ 8 kHz, Q 8: per-64 differs by **−18 dB** relative (a 750 Hz staircase,
     clearly audible as buzz on top of the wobble); DF1 vs TDF2 (both per-sample) differ by −46 dB. No
     blow-ups at this rate. The real torture test (50 Hz, 100 Hz ↔ 15 kHz) is L1.10's.

**Exit criteria:** measured vs computed within 0.1 dB ✅ (test). One-pole and biquad filters with tests ✅
(5 new test cases, 51 generated combinations; the whole suite passes in Debug and Release). Poles and zeros: explained in `biquad.h` and below ✅.

## How it works
- **One-pole** (`core/include/fss/dsp/one_pole.h`): `lp += a·(x − lp)`, `hp = x − lp`. The pole is at
  `z = 1 − a`; `a = 1 − e^(−2π·fc/fs)` puts it exactly where the sampled analog RC's decay would
  (impulse invariance). `responseAt(hz)` evaluates `a / (1 − (1−a)·z⁻¹)` at `z⁻¹ = e^{−jω}`.
- **Biquad** (`core/include/fss/dsp/biquad.h`): `designBiquad(type, fs, fc, Q, gainDb)` → `BiquadCoeffs`
  (plain doubles, RBJ cookbook), `responseAt(coeffs, hz, fs)` with `std::complex<double>`, `poleRadius`
  for a stability check, and two processing structures, `BiquadDf1<T>` and `BiquadTdf2<T>`. `Biquad`
  = `BiquadDf1<float>`.
- **Poles and zeros, at the "what do they do to the spectrum" level:** picture the unit circle as the
  frequency axis (angle 0 = DC, angle π = Nyquist). A **zero** near the circle at some angle pulls the
  response down at that frequency (on the circle: to exactly zero, the notch). A **pole** near the circle
  pushes it up (a resonance), and the closer it gets, the taller and narrower the peak and the longer the
  filter rings. RBJ's low-pass puts both zeros at Nyquist (z = −1: that's why `b = [1 2 1]·k`) and the two
  poles at the cutoff's angle; Q sets how close to the circle the poles are (radius 0.968 at 1 kHz, Q 2).
- Files: `one_pole.h`, `biquad.h`, `tests/test_filters.cpp`, `tools/render/l1_9.cpp`,
  `tools/analyze/plot_response.py` (new: measured IR spectrum vs computed CSV).

## Alternatives rejected
- **TDF2 as the default** (the textbook float choice): measured 2–4 dB noisier than DF1 here, and its state
  means less when coefficients move. The difference is small; I'd revisit if two floats of state per
  filter ever mattered (they won't: the synth filter is the SVF).
- **Double everywhere.** Fixes the 20 Hz coefficient problem, costs nothing on scalar x64 code, but halves
  SIMD width later (L6.2). Use `BiquadDf1<double>` where it matters (DC blockers, sub-bass EQ).
- **A Python `plot_response.py` that computes H from coefficients.** Then the "computed" and "measured" curves
  would both come from Python. Computing in C++ (`responseAt`) and *measuring* the real C++ filter keeps
  the test honest: the same code that runs in the synth is what's checked.
- **Per-block coefficient updates** as a CPU saving: harmless for slow sweeps (−69 dB), audible buzz for
  fast ones (−18 dB). If L2.6 profiling says coefficient math is costly, update every N samples *and*
  interpolate, or switch structure (L1.10).

## What surprised me
1. **The float-precision "noise" at 20 Hz was mostly not noise.** DF1 and TDF2 came out identical, which
   rounding noise never is. Splitting the error showed coefficient quantization 14 dB above either
   structure's state noise. A float 20 Hz high-pass is fine as a DC blocker; it's just not *exactly* 20 Hz.
2. **Slow sweeps don't zipper at 64-sample updates.** I expected tens of dB; it's −69 dB. Zipper needs a
   fast *rate of change*, not just a coarse update grid.
3. **My plot script disagreed with my test** (0.107 dB at the notch). The test was right: interpolating FFT
   bins onto a curve that falls 60 dB in a few hertz overstates the error. Good reminder that the
   measurement tool needs as much suspicion as the thing measured.

## [THINK] answers
- **Why does a biquad dislike fast modulation?** Each *frozen* biquad is stable, but a biquad whose
  coefficients change every sample is a different, time-varying system, and its states (TDF2: partial sums
  built with old coefficients; DF1: past samples, better) don't correspond to any physical energy. Rapid
  coefficient changes therefore inject transients, and in the worst case (fast, deep, high Q) the
  time-varying system can grow even though every snapshot is stable. At 8 Hz I measured only small
  structure differences (−46 dB) and no blow-ups; L1.10 pushes to 50 Hz and 15 kHz.
- **Why `1 − exp(...)` and not `fc/fs`?** See `one_pole.h`: it's the exact per-sample decay of the analog RC
  (impulse invariance); `2π·fc/fs` is its first Taylor term, measured +7 % off at 1 kHz and unstable above fs/π.

## Compare with your version
- **Your 0.1 dB test:** do you measure the response by running the actual filter (impulse → DFT, or a sine
  sweep), or compute both curves from the same formula? Only the first can catch a bug in `tick()`.
- **Float vs double coefficients:** run your 20 Hz LP in float and in double on the same noise and diff them.
  Is your error coefficient quantization or state noise? (Hint: DF1 and TDF2 identical ⇒ coefficients.)
- **Which form did you pick, and why?** Mine is DF1 by a 2–4 dB margin; TDF2 is a perfectly good answer.
