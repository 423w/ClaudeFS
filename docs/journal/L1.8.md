# L1.8: Harmonics, classic waveforms & aliasing (PolyBLEP) (CFS)
*Date started / finished:* 2026-11-17 / 2026-11-17  ·  *Built by:* Claude

## Goal
Saw, square and triangle oscillators: naive first (to hear and measure aliasing), then band-limited with
PolyBLEP (edges) and PolyBLAMP (corners), against an additive ground truth. Measure alias energy per method
per pitch, and the cost of a runtime `switch` vs a `template <Waveform W>` tick.

## Predictions (before building)
Worked out by hand from the Fourier series, before writing any C++ or rendering anything.

**Naive waveforms.** Sampling the ideal (infinite-bandwidth) waveform folds every harmonic above Nyquist
back into the band. So the alias power of a naive waveform ≈ the power of all its harmonics above
H = fs/(2·f0). Saw: harmonic h has amplitude 2/(πh), power 2/(π²h²); total power 1/3; tail above H ≈ 2/(π²H).
Relative to the total: **6/(π²H)**. Square (odd h, power 8/(π²h²), total 1): **4/(π²H)**.
Triangle (odd h, amplitude 8/(π²h²), total 1/3): **16/(π⁴H³)**.

| f0 | H | naive saw | naive square | naive triangle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ≈ 220 Hz | 109 | −22.5 dB | −24.3 dB | −83 dB |
| ≈ 1 kHz | 24 | −16.0 dB | −17.7 dB | −63 dB |
| ≈ 3 kHz | 8 | −11.2 dB | −13.0 dB | −49 dB |
| ≈ 6 kHz | 4 | −8.2 dB | −10.0 dB | −40 dB |

The saw's aliasing falls only ~3 dB per octave of pitch (1/H), the triangle's ~9 dB (1/H³): a slope corner
is much gentler than a jump.

**PolyBLEP.** It replaces each jump with a jump smeared by a 2-sample triangle kernel, so the spectrum of
the correction rolls off like sinc², about 12 dB/octave faster than the bare jump. I expect it to remove
most of the alias energy at low and mid pitches and little near Nyquist: roughly **−50 dB at 220 Hz,
−40 dB at 1 kHz, −25 dB at 3 kHz and only −15 dB at 6 kHz** for the saw (a 20–30 dB improvement at mid
pitches, shrinking at the top). The triangle with PolyBLAMP: −90 dB or better at mid pitches.
Side effect: the same kernel also attenuates the *real* top harmonics a little (a few dB in the last
octave below Nyquist), so PolyBLEP is slightly duller than additive.

**Spectrograms.** Naive sweep: a forest of lines going *down* (and bouncing between 0 and Nyquist) from the
first second on, dense and loud in the last two seconds. PolyBLEP: the same lines exist but 20–40 dB
quieter, visible mostly in the top octave. Additive: only the harmonic fan, nothing else.

**Timing.** The template tick lets the compiler drop the switch; the runtime switch is a perfectly predicted
branch in a loop that never changes waveform. I predict **no measurable difference** (< 10%) in a loop that
calls one waveform repeatedly.

## How it works
A naive saw is perfect except at one point per cycle, the jump. The jump is what has infinitely many
harmonics. PolyBLEP leaves the rest of the waveform alone: on the one sample before the jump and the one
after it, it adds a small polynomial that turns the instant jump into a quick, smooth S-curve.

```
naive saw            PolyBLEP residual (for a jump of −2)       result
   /|   /|                  ·                                     /\    /\
  / |  / |      −      ____/|  ____          =                   /  \  /  \    the 2 samples around
 /  | /  |                  |/                                  /    \/    \   each jump are bent
/   |/   |                  ·
```
Where the polynomial comes from: an ideal band-limited jump is the integral of a sinc. Replace the sinc by
the crudest low-pass kernel that still has area 1, a triangle 2 samples wide; its integral is a piecewise
quadratic, and that minus the naive step is the residual (header comment of `waveforms.h`). Integrate once
more and you get PolyBLAMP for slope corners: D(x) = (1+x)³/6 before the corner, (1−x)³/6 after. I checked
this numerically (`polyBlamp` vs a 20000-step integral of `polyBlep/2`, max error < 1e-6) and by sign: with
the BLAMP sign flipped, the triangle aliases 5 dB *more* than naive.

The triangle's corner correction is scaled by the slope change *per sample*, 8·dt. That's also why the
triangle improves less in total (12 dB): its corners are already gentle, so the remaining alias energy is
dominated by what PolyBLAMP's short kernel can't reach, just below Nyquist.

## Alternatives rejected
- **Additive** (rendered as ground truth). Exact, but costs one sine per harmonic: 240 at 100 Hz. It also
  needs harmonics faded near Nyquist, or a sweep clicks faintly each time one drops out (see "surprised").
  Right for a wavetable *generator*, wrong per sample per voice.
- **BLIT** `[STILSON-BLIT]`: generate a band-limited impulse train (closed-form sinc sum) and integrate it
  to get a saw. Nearly alias-free, but the leaky integrator brings DC drift and state that misbehaves under
  fast frequency changes, and square/triangle need two integrators. More moving parts than PolyBLEP.
- **minBLEP**: a precomputed table of a minimum-phase band-limited step, mixed in at each jump. Better
  suppression than PolyBLEP (it's a real low-pass, not a 2-sample triangle), but needs a table, an overlap
  buffer of ~16–32 samples per oscillator, and fractional-delay bookkeeping. A good upgrade if PolyBLEP's
  near-Nyquist residue ever matters (unlikely for an FM synth whose main voice is sines).
- **Oversampling** the naive waveform ×4–×16 then decimating: simple concept, but the decimation filter
  costs more than PolyBLEP, and a naive saw is so bright that even ×8 still aliases at high notes.
- **Triangle as a leaky-integrated PolyBLEP square** (the curriculum's other option). Classic, but the
  integrator's gain must track frequency (amplitude ∝ 1/f), its leak leaves a DC offset after pitch
  changes, and it has state that `reset()` must handle. PolyBLAMP is stateless, a pure function of the
  phase like the saw and square, so it can't drift.
- **`float` math in the residuals.** The polynomials run in `double` (the phase is already converted to
  double); float would save little, and t/dt near t ≈ 0 likes the precision.

## What surprised me
1. **PolyBLEP's alias reduction is lopsided.** 16 dB overall, 48 dB below 5 kHz. I expected a uniform
   20–30 dB. Once you see where the residue sits ([THINK] #1), it's exactly what the kernel predicts.
2. **The runtime switch was measurably slower** (~0.8 ns/sample), even with a perfectly predictable
   branch. I predicted no difference.
3. **The 94 Hz hum under a naive 6 kHz saw**, only 18 dB below the note. "Aliasing is high-frequency
   fizz" is wrong: aliases land anywhere, including in the bass.
4. **The additive "ground truth" isn't perfect in a sweep:** each time a harmonic crosses Nyquist and is
   dropped, it leaves a faint broadband click (the vertical stripes at ≈ −90 dBFS in the bottom panel of
   `l1.8_saw_3way.png`, ever more frequent towards 6 s). Inaudible here, but a real additive synth fades
   its top harmonics out.
5. **A vertical stripe near 0.85 s appears in all three spectrograms.** Not the oscillator: there
   f0 ≈ 187.5 Hz = fs/256, so every harmonic sits exactly on a bin of the 2048-point FFT and the leakage
   between harmonics briefly vanishes. An analyzer artifact; ask "is it in all of them?" before blaming DSP.
6. My own arithmetic slip in the triangle predictions (formula right, numbers wrong).

## [THINK] answers
- **PolyBLEP doesn't remove all aliasing. Where does the residue live, and is it audible?** Its kernel is a
  triangle 2 samples wide, whose spectrum is sinc²(f/fs): about −8 dB at Nyquist and a null at fs. So
  harmonics *just above* Nyquist (between fs/2 and fs) are barely attenuated, and they fold to *just below*
  Nyquist. Harmonics near fs (and its multiples), the ones that would fold down to *low* frequencies, sit
  near the kernel's nulls and are strongly attenuated. Result: the residue piles up in the top few kHz
  (roughly 16–24 kHz at 48 kHz), where the lattice in the PolyBLEP spectrogram is brightest, while below
  5 kHz it's 70–80 dB under the signal. Audible? Mostly not: it sits where most adults hear little, it's
  masked by the real top harmonics, and at 44.1 kHz it moves down only slightly. It can become audible when a
  later nonlinearity (distortion, a resonant filter peaking at 15 kHz, ring mod) intermodulates it down,
  or for very high notes (above ~4 kHz) where it's under 20 dB below the fundamental. Then: oversample,
  use minBLEP, or a 4-point PolyBLEP.
- **Can you apply PolyBLEP to an FM operator's output? Why not?** PolyBLEP needs to know *where* the
  discontinuities are and *how big* they are, analytically, from the phase ("the saw jumps by −2 when the
  phase wraps"). An FM operator's output is sin(phase + modulation): it has no discontinuities at all. Its
  aliasing comes from *sidebands* (carrier ± n·modulator) reaching past Nyquist: a smooth signal whose
  spectrum is simply too wide. No jump, nothing to correct. (If the carrier were a saw, its jumps would
  happen at *modulated* phase positions; PolyBLEP could still smooth them using the instantaneous phase
  increment, but the FM sidebands themselves would still alias.) FM aliasing is managed by keeping
  indices sane at high notes (key scaling, as the DX7 does) or by oversampling. That's an L4.x problem.

## Decisions made
None due in L1.8. Small calls: band-limited math in `double` from the uint32 phase (D5); triangle by
PolyBLAMP (stateless) rather than an integrated square; `BlepOscillator` clamps frequency to 0..fs/2.

## Experiments to try
1. **Listen to only the aliases**: in numpy, subtract `l1.8_sweep_saw_additive.wav` from
   `l1.8_sweep_saw_naive.wav` (same phase, so the difference is exactly the aliases), write it out and play
   it. Then the same for the PolyBLEP sweep (you'll mostly hear PolyBLEP's top-end droop, very quietly).
2. **Higher-order PolyBLEP**: replace the 2-sample residual with a 4-sample one (integrated cubic B-spline
   kernel) and re-run `fss_render l1.8`. Expect much better near-Nyquist suppression and a bit more droop.
3. **Per-block dispatch**: add `process(Waveform, float* out, int n)` that switches once and runs
   `tick<W>()` in the loop. Time it against per-sample `tick(w)`: it should match the template.
4. Render at **44.1 kHz** (change `kSampleRate`): the residue moves ~2 kHz closer to the audible band.
